Models of campaign allocation predict that parties concentrate resources in close
races. The gains from this strategy depend in part on how campaign effects vary with
the competitiveness of a constituency. I examine this question using internal data on
the UK Labour Party's 2024 general election campaign. Its campaign prioritization rule
generated two discontinuities in the allocation of additional canvassing, advertising, and
visits by high-profile politicians, allowing me to estimate campaign effects separately in
swing and stronghold seats. The campaign increased Labour's victory margin by 7.3
percentage points in strongholds but had no comparable effect in swing seats. Individuallevel
panel data show that the campaign drew previous supporters of other left-wing
parties toward Labour in both swing and stronghold seats. Previous supporters of the
Conservatives shifted toward the far right in strongholds but consolidated behind the
Conservatives in swing seats, offsetting Labour's gains. Their self-reported preferences
suggest sincere voting in strongholds but strategic voting in swing seats. Campaign
effects are therefore endogenous to a seat's electoral competitiveness, potentially limiting
the gains from concentrating resources in close races.
